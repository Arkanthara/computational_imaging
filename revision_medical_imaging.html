General Principles of Medical Imaging

Pipeline · Anatomy · Modalities · Anatomical vs Functional · Data types · Challenges

The Medical Imaging Pipeline

All medical imaging follows the same fundamental pipeline, regardless of modality:

Patient / Object
→
Imaging Device
→
Raw Data
→
Reconstruction Algorithm
→
Cross-sectional Image

Key insight: The reconstruction algorithm is the core of computational imaging. Without it, raw data (projections, signals, echoes…) cannot be directly interpreted clinically.

Anatomical Coordinates & Imaging Planes

Directions

Patient-centered axes

Superior/Inferior — up/down
Left/Right — from patient's perspective
Anterior/Posterior — front/back

Axial plane

⊥ long body axis

Separates superior from inferior. Also called transverse plane.

Sagittal plane

Left ↔ Right

Sagitta = "arrow" (Latin). Divides the body into left and right halves.

Coronal plane

Anterior ↔ Posterior

Corona = "crown" (Latin). Divides body front from back.

⚠️ Left and right are always defined from the patient's point of view, not the observer's — a common source of confusion when reading images.

Anatomical vs Functional Imaging

Anatomical imaging (e.g. MRI brain)

  • Shows physical structure of organs
  • Independent of metabolic activity
  • Similar image in living and dead patient
  • Examples: MRI, CT, X-ray

Functional imaging (e.g. PET)

  • Captures physiological activity in real time
  • Bright zones = high metabolic activity
  • Shows nothing on a deceased patient
  • Examples: PET, fMRI, SPECT

💡 "An MRI shows you have a brain. A PET shows you're using it."

Medical Imaging Challenges

Dataset & acquisition challenges
  • Many modalities, very large images but small datasets — simultaneously scarce and voluminous
  • Non-standardized acquisition — high variability between devices, protocols, configurations
  • Long-tail disease distribution — rare diseases make learning difficult
  • Rare and noisy labels — annotating images requires rare expertise, expensive
  • Heterogeneous and imbalanced samples — far more normal than pathological cases
  • Subjectivity in ground truth — experts can disagree on annotations
  • Anonymization sometimes impossible — e.g. facial scans can identify individuals
Learning strategies to address challenges
  • Unsupervised learning (e.g. GAN) — reconstruct next healthy MRI slices, detect anomalies via L₂ loss
  • Supervised learning — patch-level labels, direct image→label mapping
  • Semi-supervised learning — mix of labeled and unlabeled data
  • Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) — weak image-level labels; image split into patches (bags of instances); positive bag = at least one positive instance. Useful in digital pathology.
  • Transfer learning — transfer knowledge from data-rich domain A to data-scarce domain B

Radiography

X-ray physics · Interactions with matter · Detectors · Applications (fluoroscopy, angiography, DSA)

Historical Context

Radiography is the oldest medical imaging modality. Key dates:

  • 1895: Wilhelm Conrad Röntgen discovers X-rays ("Über eine neue Art von Strahlen"). First published image: hand of Anna Bertha Röntgen.
  • 1901: Röntgen receives the first Nobel Prize in Physics.

⚠️ Clinical use began before the dangers of ionizing radiation were understood — a recurring lesson in medical technology history. Röntgen died of intestinal cancer.

X-ray Physics

X-rays are electromagnetic waves (photons). Their energy is:

E = h·f = hc/λ   |   h = 4.135×10⁻¹⁵ eVs  ·  Energy ≈ keV range

On the EM spectrum: between UV and gamma rays. Short wavelength (~10⁻¹⁰ m) → high penetrating power.

X-ray generation in the tube

Electrons are accelerated from cathode to anode and release energy as Bremsstrahlung (braking radiation) → continuous emission spectrum + characteristic peaks at orbital energy levels.

  • Photon quantity: 6–100 mA
  • Photon energy: determined by voltage Vcathode-anode = 50–125 kV

Interactions of X-rays with Matter

InteractionMechanismEffect on imageDesirability
Photoelectric absorption Photon fully absorbed → electron ejected Dense tissue (bone) absorbs more → white. Less dense (air) → dark. Controls contrast. ✓ Most desirable
Compton scattering Partial absorption → photon deflected + electron ejected Deflected photon detected as noise (non-linear ray). Degrades contrast. ✗ Less desirable
Pair production Energy >1.02 MeV → electron-positron pair → annihilation → 2 opposite photons Used in PET, not conventional X-ray (requires very high energy) Used in nuclear imaging

X-ray Detectors

Photographic film

Legacy

Only 1–2% of radiation stopped. Inefficient → unnecessarily high patient dose.

Intensifying screens

Phosphor-based

Phosphor converts X-ray photons → light photons. Efficiency 5–20%. Types: fluorescence (<10⁻⁸ s) vs phosphorescence (delayed).

XRII

X-ray Image Intensifier

Al/Ti entrance window. Phosphor absorbs X-rays → electrons → photocathode. Brightness amplified ~10⁵×.

Image Quality Factors

  • Quality of anode tip (finer = better focus)
  • Patient thickness (thicker = more scatter = lower resolution)
  • Phosphor light scatter properties
  • Film resolution / digital sampling / laser spot size
  • Typical resolution: screen-film 5–15 lp/mm (100–33 µm) | Storage phosphors 2.5–5 lp/mm
  • Mammography requires direct radiography for highest resolution; image size ≥ 2000² pixels

Applications

Fluoroscopy — real-time X-ray imaging
  • Chain: X-ray source → patient → fluorescent → recorder
  • Uses XRII: brightness ~10⁵× brighter output, flux gain ≈ 100, minification gain ≈ 100
  • Limitation: quantum noise (few photons)
  • Modern: flat-panel detectors → better sensitivity, less dose, better temporal resolution
  • C-arm: portable fluoroscope, rotates around surgical table, produces digital images; uses limited projections for 2D reconstruction
Angiography — visualizing blood vessels
  • Blood has same X-ray density as surrounding tissue → need contrast
  • Iodine-based contrast agent injected into bloodstream
  • Detects: aneurysms (localized arterial wall weakness), vascular leaks, thromboses (blood clots)
  • Can be static images or video sequences (25–30 fps)
  • Retinal angiography: identifies vessel narrowing in diabetic retinopathy and macular degeneration
Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA)
  • Mask image acquired before contrast injection
  • Fluoroscopy produces images at 1–6 fps; all subsequent images are subtracted from the mask in real time
  • Result: only contrast-enhanced vessels remain visible — surrounding structures eliminated
  • ⚠️ DSA is increasingly replaced by CT angiography (less invasive, less stressful for patient)

Computed Tomography (CT)

History · Radon transform · FBP · Beam geometries · CT types · Quality · Advantages / disadvantages

History — Three Independent Discoveries

1917

Johann Radon

Mathematical framework for tomography — the Radon transform.

1963

Allan Cormack

Mathematical analysis of tomographic reconstruction — independently of Radon.

1972

Godfrey Hounsfield

First CT scanner — independently of both Radon and Cormack. Own reconstruction method.

1979

Nobel Prize

Hounsfield + Cormack — Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

💡 Three people independently developed the same mathematical framework — an illustration of convergent ideas in the history of science.

How CT Works

Unlike standard X-ray (cone beam 3D), CT collimates rays into a 2D fan beam:

X-ray source + fan beam
→
Many detector projections
→
Rotate around patient
→
Sinogram
→
Reconstruction
→
3D volume

The sinogram = one line per projection angle. It encodes all projection data.

Mathematical Reconstruction

Radon Transform

The collected data are integrals (projections) of the attenuation function μ(x,y):

p_θ(r,θ) = ∫∫ μ(x,y) · δ(x·cos θ + y·sin θ − r) dx dy

Projection-Slice Theorem

  • Infinite projections ⇒ perfect reconstruction of μ(x,y)
  • Inverse Radon transform ⇒ estimates μ(x,y)
  • Unstable with noisy data

Filtered Back-Projection (FBP)

The stabilized, discretized version of the inverse Radon transform. Standard algorithm in CT reconstruction.

⚠️ In practice, only a finite number of projections exist → finite resolution artifacts.

Beam Geometries

GeometryDescriptionPracticality
Parallel-beamNew source position per rayNot practical
Fan-beam (2D)Single source → 1D detector row. One shot per angle.Standard CT
Cone-beam (3D)Single source → 2D flat detector. One rotation = full 3D.Cone-beam CT (CBCT)

CT Acquisition Modes

Single-slit CT — simplest
  • Produces one axial slice per acquisition
  • Step-and-shoot axial mode: table moves between slices
Helical / Spiral CT — most common clinically
  • X-ray tube + detectors rotate while patient moves along z-axis
  • Fast 3D data acquisition
  • Slightly lower z-resolution than step-and-shoot
  • Can tilt detector ±30°
  • Pitch = Table Feed / Δz
Multi-slice CT — near real-time 3D
  • Multiple detector rows → cone of X-ray data → 2D projection of 3D patient
  • 1–3 rotations/second → quasi real-time 3D imaging at up to 20 cm/sec translation
Cone-beam CT (CBCT) — single rotation 3D
  • 2D flat detector; only one rotation needed
  • Reconstruction: Feldkamp-Davis-Kress (FDK) equation
  • Back-projection along cone-beam rays
  • Advantages: potentially very fast, used for 3D angiography
  • Disadvantages: sampling issues at extremities, varying z sampling rate

CT Image Quality

Acquisition

Geometry

Focal spot size, detector element size, table feed, interpolation method, sampling distance

Reconstruction

Algorithm

Reconstruction kernel (filter), interpolation, voxel size

Noise

Quantum noise

Statistical nature of X-rays. More power = less noise but higher dose. Larger Δz = less noise but lower axial resolution.

Contrast

Physical factors

X-ray spectrum, beam hardening, scatter

Advantages & Disadvantages

Advantages

  • Eliminates superposition of structures outside ROI
  • Small differences in physical density can be distinguished
  • Multiplanar reformatted imaging (axial / coronal / sagittal)
  • CT angiography avoids invasive arterial catheter
  • CT colonoscopy: similar sensitivity to barium enema, potentially lower dose

Disadvantages

  • Moderate to high radiation dose
  • CT = only 7% of radiological exams (UK) but contributes 47% of collective medical X-ray dose
  • Contrast agents: risk of allergic reactions, nephropathy (kidney damage)
  • Higher risk of nephropathy in patients with renal insufficiency, diabetes
  • For moderate renal insufficiency: prefer MRI over CT

Nuclear Imaging: SPECT vs PET

Tracers · SPECT principle · PET principle · Comparison table

General Principle of Nuclear Imaging

Nuclear imaging is a form of metabolic/functional imaging. The general workflow:

Inject radiotracer
→
Tracer reaches metabolic target
→
Tracer emits radiation
→
Detect photons externally
→
Reconstruct 3D functional image

💡 Tracers are molecule-specific. Example: deprenyl triggers dopamine production. High metabolic activity = bright zones in the image.

Like fMRI, nuclear imaging is metabolic — but with much higher SNR (several orders of magnitude better than fMRI).

SPECT — Single Photon Emission CT

Principle

  • Mainly used to study blood flow
  • Radiopharmaceutical injected (or inhaled/ingested)
  • Gamma camera acquires a 2D view of 3D radionuclide distribution
  • Camera rotates 360° around patient → multiple 2D projections
  • Tomographic reconstruction → 3D dataset
  • Projections every 3–6°, 15–20 sec each → total scan 15–20 min

SPECT cameras

  • Pinhole gamma camera rotates around patient
  • Multi-head cameras: 2-head (2 simultaneous projections) or 3-head (120° spacing) → faster acquisition
  • Resolution: 64×64 or 128×128 pixels (low); pixel size 3–6 mm
  • Reconstructed images: lower resolution, increased noise, reconstruction artifacts vs planar

SPECT + CT hybrid

Modern SPECT devices are integrated with CT scanners:

  • CT images serve as an attenuation map of tissues
  • Integrated into SPECT reconstruction to correct for attenuation
  • Co-registered CT provides anatomical information

⚠️ Without attenuation correction, deep activity is underestimated in SPECT.

PET — Positron Emission Tomography

Physical principle — positron annihilation

Inject ¹⁸F-FDG
→
¹⁸F decays → emits positron (β⁺)
→
Positron annihilates with electron
→
2 gamma photons (180° apart)
→
Coincidence detection
→
3D reconstruction

Key: Only simultaneous (coincident) photon pairs are used for reconstruction — no collimator needed. This gives PET its superior sensitivity and resolution over SPECT.

PET clinical applications

Cardiology

Heart & vessels

Myocardial viability, coronary artery disease

Oncology

Cancer / Tumors

Tumor detection, staging, treatment response (FDG avid tumors)

Neurology

Nervous system

Brain activity, Alzheimer's, epilepsy, Parkinson's

Why iterative reconstruction is preferred in nuclear imaging

  • Limited number of projections
  • Irregular angle spacing and varied angles
  • Statistical (Poisson) noise and scattering
  • Non-linear ray paths
  • Need for metal artifact correction during reconstruction

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

History · Physics (T1, T2) · Image formation · Modes (fMRI, MRA, MRS, EPI) · MRI vs CT

History

1946

Felix Bloch & Edward Purcell

Stanford + Harvard. Demonstrate Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR).

1973

Paul Lauterbur

Stony Brook University. Publishes first MRI image in Nature. Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 2003.

Late 1970s

First human images

First human MRI images designed.

1993

fMRI

Functional MRI demonstrated in humans.

Physical Basis of MRI

Protons align with B₀
→
RF pulse at resonance frequency ν
→
Protons → high energy state
→
Precession → new RF signal emitted
→
Signal captured by antenna

Resonance frequency (Larmor frequency)

ν = γ · |B₀|    |    γ (hydrogen) = 42.58 MHz/T

Key insight: T1 and T2 are intrinsic properties of tissues, different per tissue type. These differences create contrast in MRI — the main source of its diagnostic richness.

T1 and T2 Relaxation

T1 — Spin-lattice relaxation

  • Return of longitudinal magnetization M_z to equilibrium M₀
  • Equation: M_z = M₀(1 − e^{−t/T₁})
  • T₁ = time to reduce difference (M_z − M₀) by factor e
  • Describes how protons give energy back to the lattice

T2 — Spin-spin relaxation

  • Return of transverse magnetization M_xy to equilibrium (→ 0)
  • Equation: M_xy = M_xy0 · e^{−t/T₂}
  • Always: T2 ≤ T1
  • Both T1 and T2 processes occur simultaneously
  • When t→∞: M_xy→0 and M_z→M₀

Image Formation

  • Gradients are added to B₀ → spatial variation of B₀(r) → only selected parts resonate at transmission frequency ν
  • Tomographic methods (back-projection, etc.) generate 2D images
  • Stronger gradients → faster imaging or higher resolution
  • Faster gradient switching → faster scan (limited by nerve stimulation safety concerns)
  • Typical resolution: ~1 mm³

MRI Modes

Standard MRI — pulse sequences

Uses different RF pulse sequences (time series of RF pulses) to manipulate magnetization vector M. Choice of sequence determines T1-weighted, T2-weighted, or proton-density-weighted contrast.

Echo-Planar Imaging (EPI)
  • Each RF excitation followed by a train of gradients with different spatial encodings
  • Fast data collection → fewer motion artifacts
  • Foundation for fMRI
MR Spectroscopy (MRS)
  • Images nuclei other than H: P (phosphorus), Na (sodium), F (fluorine)
  • MRI shows where a tumor is; MRS shows how aggressive it is (malignancy)
  • Applications: brain/breast/prostate cancer, epilepsy, Parkinson's, Huntington's disease
Functional MRI (fMRI) — BOLD contrast
  • Measures changes in cerebral blood flow and oxygenation
  • Active neurons consume oxygen transported by hemoglobin
  • Oxyhemoglobin = diamagnetic; Deoxyhemoglobin = paramagnetic
  • MR signal of blood differs by oxygenation level → BOLD contrast (Blood-Oxygen-Level Dependent)
MR Angiography (MRA)
  • Images blood vessels: evaluates stenosis (abnormal narrowing) and aneurysms
  • Techniques: paramagnetic contrast agent injection OR flow-related enhancement (fresh blood entering the plane responds differently from static tissue)
Interventional MRI
  • MRI scanner guides minimally invasive procedures simultaneously
  • Strong magnetic RF field → requires non-magnetic instruments
  • Open magnet design for better surgeon access
  • Typically uses weaker field (~0.2 T) → reduced sensitivity
Current Density Imaging (CDI)
  • Maps electrical current pathways through tissue
  • External current applied during MRI → magnetic fields affect phase of magnetic dipoles
  • CDI uses MRI phase information to reconstruct current densities inside the object

Medical Ultrasound Imaging

History · Physics · A/M/B modes · Doppler (CW & PW) · Applications

Historical Milestones

  • 1877: Lord Rayleigh — The Theory of Sound
  • 1880: Pierre Curie — discovery of the piezoelectric effect (enables generation and detection of ultrasonic waves)
  • WWI: First practical use — submarine detection
  • 1942: First clinical use — brain tumor localization (Karl & Friederich Dussik)
  • 1950: First grayscale images
  • 1965: Real-time imaging by Siemens device
  • 1968: Electronic beam steering with phased-array technology
  • Mid-1970s: Popular technique; substantial improvements since mid-1990s

Properties of Ultrasound

Advantages

  • Non-invasive
  • Inexpensive
  • Portable
  • Excellent temporal resolution
  • No radiation — safe in pregnancy

Disadvantages

  • Noisy (significant speckle noise)
  • Low spatial resolution
  • Operator-dependent
  • Dependent on patient anatomy

Physical Properties of Ultrasonic Waves

Ultrasonic waves are longitudinal compression waves:

  • Transducer emits a sound pulse → compresses the medium
  • Elasticity limits compression → extends into rarefaction
  • Rarefaction returns to compression → oscillation continues until damping stops it
  • Particles never travel far — energy is transmitted, not matter

Medical ultrasound frequency: > 2.5 MHz (beyond the ~20 Hz – 20 kHz human hearing range). "Ultra" = beyond audible.

Acquisition Modes

Mode A (Amplitude) — simplest, now obsolete
  • Principle: clap and listen for echo
  • Distance formula: d = (elapsed time × speed of sound) / 2
  • Speed of sound ≈ constant in tissues → time ≈ amplitude
  • Problem: only 1 line of information — unknown direction, unknown shape
Mode M (Motion) — repeated A-mode
  • Very high sampling rate: up to 1,000 pulses/second
  • Single axis tracked over time → visualizes movement of a structure (e.g. heart wall)
  • Still extensively used in cardiac and fetal imaging
Mode B (Brightness) — standard 2D imaging
  • B = Brightness
  • Obtained by translating or tilting the transducer
  • Multiple A-mode lines at different orientations → assembled into a 2D image
  • Applications: fetal imaging, cardiac imaging (normal and continuous)

Doppler Effect — Measuring Blood Flow Velocity

When a source approaches → waves are compressed (higher frequency). When it moves away → waves are stretched (lower frequency). This shift encodes velocity.

Continuous Wave (CW) Doppler
  • Compares transmitted frequency f_T with received frequency f_R
  • Doppler frequency: f_D = f_R − f_T ≈ −2|v_a|cos(θ) / c · f_T
  • where v_a = blood velocity, θ = angle between beam and flow, c = speed of sound
  • Doppler signal can be made audible — pitch ∝ velocity
Pulsed Wave (PW) Doppler — not strictly Doppler
  • Received signal assumed = scaled, delayed replica of transmitted: s(t) = A·sin(2πf_T(t − Δt))
  • Δt = time between transmission and reception (depends on transducer-to-scatterer distance)
  • One sample acquired per received pulse at time t_R
  • If scatterer moves at velocity v_a → distance increases by v_a·T_PRF per pulse repetition period → increases Δt

Clinical Applications

3D Ultrasound

Fetal 3D imaging

Visualizes fetal face in 3D. Widely used in obstetrics.

IVUS

Intravascular Ultrasound

Miniaturized transducer introduced into a blood vessel to image its wall from inside.

Doppler

Blood flow mapping

Red = flow toward transducer. Blue = flow away. Used in vascular, cardiac imaging.

US CT

Breast mammography

Ultrasound CT used as alternative approach to breast imaging.

💡 Ultrasound is the only medical imaging modality routinely used for pregnancy monitoring — thanks to its safety (no radiation) and portability.

Parameters of Optical Microscopes
FLASH MEMO — Rapid Revision
  • NA = n·sin(θ) — governs resolution and light collection
  • OTF = FT{PSF} — disc of radius k_max in Fourier space
  • k_max = NA × 2π/λ — max spatial frequency transmitted
  • High NA ↑ resolution ↑ but FOV ↓ — fundamental trade-off
  • Resolution limit: ~λ/2NA (Abbe criterion)
Numerical Aperture (NA) OTF / PSF Resolution vs FOV k_max Fourier space diffraction limit

1. Numerical Aperture (NA)

The numerical aperture is the single most important parameter of a microscope objective. It determines both the spatial resolution and the light collection efficiency.

NA = n · sin(θ) n = refractive index of the medium (air=1, oil~1.515, water~1.33) θ = half-angle of acceptance of the objective

Low NA Example — 2× objective

  • NA = 0.08 (θ ≈ 4.6°)
  • Field of view: ~1 mm diameter
  • Low resolution — coarse structures only
  • Good for overview, poor for cell detail

High NA Example — 20× objective

  • NA = 0.4 (θ ≈ 23.6°)
  • Field of view: ~100 µm
  • Good resolution — subcellular details
  • Cannot image whole tissue at once

2. Optical Transfer Function (OTF)

The OTF describes how each spatial frequency component of the object is attenuated by the optical system. It is the Fourier transform of the PSF:

OTF = FT{ PSF } = circ(k_max) · e^{iφ(kx,ky)} k_max = NA_obj × 2π/λ (radius of OTF support disc) φ(kx,ky) = wavefront aberration (φ=0 for diffraction-limited)
💡 Intuition
The OTF acts as a low-pass filter in spatial frequency. It passes all frequencies within a disc of radius k_max and blocks everything beyond. Larger NA → larger disc → better resolution.

3. Resolution / Field-of-View Trade-Off

⚠ Fundamental Limitation
A single conventional objective cannot simultaneously achieve high resolution (high NA) and large field of view (low magnification). FPM is specifically designed to overcome this.

Two-Photon Microscopy
FLASH MEMO
  • Two photons of energy hf/2 absorbed simultaneously → excites fluorophore needing energy hf
  • Uses NIR wavelengths (~700–1000 nm) → less absorption + scattering in tissue
  • Excitation only at focal point → no pinhole needed, less phototoxicity
  • Imaging depth up to ~1 mm in living tissue
  • Requires femtosecond pulsed laser for high photon density
  • Lateral resolution ~0.64 µm, axial ~3.35 µm
Two-photon absorption NIR excitation Focal confinement fs-pulsed laser Tissue scattering Ti:Sapphire laser In vivo imaging Intravital microscopy

1. Why Deep Tissue Imaging Is Challenging

Biological tissues block light in two ways: absorption and scattering. Both phenomena are strongly wavelength-dependent.

Tissue opacity vs wavelength

UV / Violet (~405nm)
Very opaque
Green (~532nm)
Limited depth
Red (~633nm)
Partial penetration
NIR (~800nm)
Best penetration

Scattering ∝ 1/λ⁴ — NIR is scattered ~20× less than blue light.

2. One-Photon vs Two-Photon Excitation

1-Photon Fluorescence

  • One photon of energy hf
  • Excitation wavelength: short (visible)
  • Excitation throughout beam path
  • Pinhole required (confocal)
  • More phototoxicity & bleaching
VS

2-Photon Fluorescence

  • Two photons of energy hf/2 each
  • Excitation wavelength: long (NIR)
  • Excitation only at focal point
  • No pinhole needed
  • Reduced phototoxicity & bleaching
💡 Key Intuition
Two-photon absorption is so statistically rare that it only occurs where photon density is extreme — at the focal point of a tightly focused femtosecond laser. This provides inherent 3D confinement without any optical filtering.

In vivo and ex vivo applications

Ex vivo (explant): tissue removed, kept at 37°C with O₂/CO₂. Used to observe T-lymphocyte behavior, cluster formation (day 1), swarming and division (day 5).

QUESTION 09
Light-Field Microscopy
FLASH MEMO
  • Light field = 4D representation — position (x,y) + direction (angle) of each ray
  • Adds a microlens array at the native image plane of a standard microscope
  • Captures a full 3D volume in one single camera snapshot
  • Trade-off: spatial resolution sacrificed for volumetric + angular info
  • Post-acquisition: numerical refocusing at any depth
4D Light Field Microlens array Single-snapshot 3D Angular sampling Numerical refocusing Plenoptic camera

1. The Light Field Concept

A standard camera sensor records only 2D intensity I(x,y). The light field extends this to a 4D representation that encodes both position and direction of each ray:

Standard Camera

  • Records I(x, y) — 2D intensity only
  • One fixed focus plane
  • No depth information

Light-Field Camera

  • Records L(x, y, u, v) — 4D: position + direction
  • Depth information encoded
  • Refocus after capture
💡 Intuition
Imagine a 2D grid of cameras all pointed at the same scene simultaneously. Each camera gives a slightly different viewpoint (parallax). The microlens array achieves this effect on a single sensor — each microlens acts as one "virtual camera."

2. LFM Architecture

Key components

1
Objective + Tube Lens
Form the native image plane of the specimen.
2
Microlens Array (MLA)
Placed exactly at the native image plane. Each microlens subtends a small spatial patch and re-images it onto a set of pixels → encodes angular direction.
3
Camera Sensor
Each pixel behind a microlens records the intensity from a specific position + direction. The full 4D light field is captured in one snapshot.
4
3D Reconstruction Algorithm
Deconvolution applied to the raw 4D data → reconstructed volume at multiple depths.
⚠ Key Trade-off
In LFM, the total pixel count is fixed. Pixels behind each microlens sample angle (depth) instead of spatial position. This means spatial resolution is sacrificed in exchange for 3D information. The more angles sampled, the lower the spatial resolution per view.

QUESTION 10
Coherent Microscope Imaging Model
FLASH MEMO
  • I(x,y) = |( O(x,y)·P(x,y) ) * PSF|² — intensity = |complex field|²
  • OTF = FT{PSF} = circ(k_max) · e^{iφ} — disc in Fourier space
  • k_max = NA × 2π/λ
  • Plane wave illumination: P(x,y) = e^{i·k_xn·x} · e^{i·k_yn·y}
  • Object O(x,y) is complex — contains both amplitude and phase

The Complete Coherent Model

I(x,y) = | ( O(x,y) · P(x,y) ) * PSF |² O(x,y) = complex object (amplitude + phase) — UNKNOWN, to recover P(x,y) = illumination field (plane wave) PSF = Point Spread Function of the objective * = convolution | · |² = intensity (squares the modulus)

The Optical Transfer Function (OTF)

Taking the Fourier transform of the PSF gives the OTF:

OTF = FT{ PSF } = circ(k_max) · e^{iφ(kx,ky)} circ(k_max) = disc of radius k_max in Fourier space (support of the OTF) φ(kx,ky) = wavefront aberration map (φ = 0 for diffraction-limited) k_max = NA_obj × 2π/λ
💡 Fourier Space Interpretation
In Fourier space, convolution with PSF becomes multiplication by OTF. The OTF is a circular low-pass filter — it passes spatial frequencies within radius k_max and zeros out everything outside. The larger the NA, the larger the disc, the more high-frequency detail is preserved.

Plane Wave Illumination (P_n)

A plane wave tilted at angle (k_xn, k_yn) is written:

P_n(x,y) = e^{i·k_xn·x} · e^{i·k_yn·y} (k_xn, k_yn) = transverse wavevector (encodes angle of incidence) For normal incidence: k_xn = k_yn = 0 → P = 1 (uniform)

Effect in Fourier Domain

FT{ O(x,y) · P_n(x,y) } = Ô(kx - k_xn, ky - k_yn) Multiplying by a plane wave = SHIFTING the spectrum by (k_xn, k_yn)
🔑 Key Insight (Foundation of FPM)
Each tilted illumination shifts the object spectrum so that a different patch of the Fourier plane is sampled by the OTF disc. Collecting many such patches allows reconstruction of the full high-frequency spectrum → high resolution.

QUESTION 11
Simplified Imaging Model
FLASH MEMO
  • For the n-th LED: I_n(x,y) = |( O(x,y)·P_n(x,y) ) * PSF|²
  • Illumination: P_n = e^{i·k_xn·x} · e^{i·k_yn·y}
  • In Fourier: n-th image accesses patch at (k_xn, k_yn) offset in spectrum
  • Goal: recover O(x,y) from all {I_n}

FPM Measurement Model

For each of the N illumination angles (one per LED), the camera records a low-resolution intensity image:

I_n(x,y) = | ( O(x,y) · P_n(x,y) ) * PSF |² n = 1, 2, 3, …, N P_n(x,y) = e^{i·k_xn·x} · e^{i·k_yn·y} ← n-th LED defines the angle (k_xn, k_yn) = transverse wavevector of n-th LED

Fourier Space Picture

Core equation in words: The n-th LED shifts the object's Fourier spectrum by (k_xn, k_yn), so the OTF disc (of fixed size k_max) captures a different circular patch of the spectrum. Combining all patches reconstructs a complete high-resolution spectrum.

The inverse problem to solve:

Recover O(x,y) from { I_n(x,y) }_{n=1..N} — O is complex (amplitude + phase), unknown — I_n are real (intensity), measured — Phase recovery is implicit in the reconstruction algorithm

QUESTION 12
FPM: Principle of Operation
FLASH MEMO
  • Use low-NA objective → large FOV but low resolution
  • Illuminate with LED matrix — each LED = different angle of incidence
  • Each angle captures a different Fourier patch of the object spectrum
  • Iterative reconstruction tiles these patches into a large synthetic aperture
  • Result: synthetic NA ~0.5 from a 2× (NA 0.08) objective, 1.2 cm FOV
  • ~137 images needed for robust reconstruction
LED Matrix Synthetic Aperture Large FOV + High Res Fourier patch tiling 137 images No mechanical scan

The Core Idea

Fourier Patch Tiling — The Mechanism

The Fourier spectrum of the object is sampled in overlapping patches. Each patch corresponds to one LED angle:

Before reconstruction

  • Single OTF disc of radius k_max (small for low NA)
  • Only the central region of the spectrum accessible
  • Low resolution image

After FPM reconstruction

  • Many overlapping discs tiled across spectrum
  • Synthetic aperture radius = NA_obj + NA_illumination
  • NA_synthetic ≈ 0.5 from NA 0.08 objective
💡 Analogy
The OTF disc is like a small flashlight. Each LED illumination shines this flashlight on a different part of the "dark" Fourier spectrum. After exploring all parts, you've built up the complete picture — a much larger synthetic aperture.
Experimental System (Zheng et al., Nature Photonics 2013)
  • Compatible with standard microscope (simple modification)
  • 2× objective, NA = 0.08
  • LED matrix below the sample
  • 137 low-resolution images acquired
  • FOV: ~1.2 cm diameter
  • Synthetic NA: ~0.5 (6× improvement)
  • No mechanical scanning required

QUESTION 13
FPM: Image Reconstruction Method
FLASH MEMO
  • Iterative algorithm alternating between image space and Fourier space
  • Key constraint: replace simulated amplitude with measured amplitude, keep phase
  • 5 steps per image, repeat for all N images (= 1 full iteration)
  • Converges in 2–3 full iterations
  • Recovers both amplitude and phase of the object

Iterative Algorithm — Step by Step

1
Initialization
Start with an interpolated low-resolution image as initial estimate of the high-resolution complex field √I_hr · e^{iφ_hr}. Compute its Fourier transform.
2
Simulate LR Image for Angle n
Extract the patch of the current HR spectrum corresponding to LED n (at offset k_xn, k_yn). Apply OTF. Inverse-FT → simulated LR image √I_l · e^{iφ_l}.
3
Replace Amplitude with Measurement
√I_l · e^{iφ_l} → √I_lm · e^{iφ_l} Keep the PHASE, replace AMPLITUDE with measurement √I_lm
The measured intensity encodes ground truth amplitude; the estimated phase is retained.
4
Update HR Spectrum
Take Fourier transform of the corrected LR image and back-project it into the corresponding patch of the HR spectrum. Only the patch at position (k_xn, k_yn) is updated.
5
Repeat for All N Images
Steps 2–4 are applied for every LED angle n = 1, 2, …, N. One full pass = one iteration. Repeat 2–3 full iterations until convergence.
Phase Imaging — Bonus Result

Because the algorithm recovers a complex object field, both amplitude and phase are simultaneously reconstructed. Phase imaging reveals cell structure without staining (cells are phase objects — they retard light without absorbing it).

  • Validated on microspheres (6.5 µm, 3 µm) — matches theoretical phase profile
  • Applied to unstained mouse kidney — structures invisible in intensity become clear in phase
  • Compared favorably to phase-shifting interferometry (reference method)

QUESTION 14
Epi-Illuminated Fourier Ptychography
FLASH MEMO
  • Standard FPM uses transmission — light through the sample
  • Epi-FP uses reflection — same objective illuminates AND detects
  • For opaque samples: metals, ICs, thick biological tissue, PCBs
  • Two concentric LED rings provide different angles
  • Achieves NA_synth = 0.76 from NA 0.28 objective (390 nm feature resolution)

Motivation — Opaque Samples

Transmission FPM requires light to pass through the sample — impossible for opaque objects (metals, semiconductor wafers, thick tissue). Epi-illuminated FP solves this by using reflected light.

Standard FPM (Transmission)

  • LED below the sample
  • Objective above collects transmitted light
  • Only for transparent samples
  • Histological slides, cell cultures
VS

Epi-FP (Reflection)

  • LED rings around the objective
  • Same objective: illuminates + collects
  • Works for opaque surfaces
  • ICs, metals, PCBs, surfaces

Configuration

Epi-FP Setup

  • LED Ring 1 (inner): small-angle incidence — accesses central Fourier region
  • LED Ring 2 (outer): large-angle incidence — accesses peripheral Fourier region
  • Same objective used for both illumination and detection
  • Reflected wavefront analyzed iteratively (same algorithm as standard FPM)

Results

ParameterInitial (direct imaging)After Epi-FP
Objective NA0.28—
Synthetic NA0.28 (no improvement)0.76
Resolution (USAF target)~1 µm390 nm (Grp 10, Elem 2)
Improvement factor—~2.7× NA improvement
Detection-Path FP (Aperture Scanning Variant)

Another variant: instead of varying illumination angle, a physical aperture is scanned across the Fourier plane in the detection path. Mathematically equivalent to FPM but with fixed illumination and variable detection aperture.

  • Object illuminated with fixed plane wave
  • Aperture scanned across pupil plane
  • Each aperture position selects a Fourier patch
  • Same iterative reconstruction algorithm applies
  • Applications: 3D holographic refocusing (±500 µm), macroscopic FP (0.7 m distance)

QUESTION 15
Spectrum Multiplexing in FPM
FLASH MEMO
  • Standard FPM: one LED at a time → slow
  • Multiplexing: R, G, B LEDs lit simultaneously → faster acquisition
  • Measured intensity = sum of contributions from all colors
  • Algorithm separates color channels via proportional update rule
  • Only a monochrome sensor needed — color recovered computationally
RGB simultaneous illumination Monochrome sensor Computational color Proportional update Faster acquisition

The Multiplexed Measurement Model

I_n(x,y) = Σ_i | ( O_i(x,y) · P_n(x,y) ) * PSF_i |² i = color channel (R, G, B) O_i = complex object at wavelength i PSF_i = PSF at wavelength i Goal: recover O_R, O_G, O_B from monochrome I_n

5-Step Algorithm for Spectrum Multiplexing

1
Initialize 3 separate HR images
Start with estimates √I_r·e^{iφ_r}, √I_g·e^{iφ_g}, √I_b·e^{iφ_b} independently for each color.
2
Generate target LR images per channel
For angle n, extract patch from each color's HR spectrum and compute simulated LR intensities I_rt, I_gt, I_bt.
3
Sum the channel intensities
I_t = I_rt + I_gt + I_bt ← simulated total intensity
4
Proportional amplitude update
I'_rt = (I_m / I_t) · I_rt ← scale red channel by measurement ratio Likewise for G and B. Phase of each channel is preserved.
Each channel is updated proportionally to its share of the total signal.
5
Update HR spectra
Back-project corrected LR images (per channel) into the respective HR spectra at offset (k_xn, k_yn).
💡 Why This Works
The proportional update assumes that each channel's contribution to the total measured signal is proportional to its simulated intensity. If red is dominant, it gets the most of the measurement correction. Over many iterations, the colors separate cleanly.

Advantages

Speed

Acquire R, G, B simultaneously → 3× fewer exposures needed.

Low Cost

Standard monochrome CCD — no color filter, no Bayer mosaic.

No Chromatic Filter

Color separation done computationally, not optically.

Partial Coherence Extension

LEDs are spatially extended sources, not point sources — they have partial coherence. To model this, the illumination is treated as a sum of many plane waves with slightly different angles:

I_n(x,y) = Σ_{Δx,Δy} | ( O(x,y) · e^{i(k_xn+Δx)x} · e^{i(k_yn+Δy)y} ) * PSF |² The sum models the angular extent of the LED (incoherent sum of coherent contributions)

QUESTION 16
Sampling Strategy in FPM
FLASH MEMO
  • Object spectrum is not uniform — energy concentrated at low frequencies (center)
  • Optimal: denser sampling near center, sparser at periphery
  • Uniform LED grid → higher RMS error, slower convergence
  • Non-uniform sampling (annular rings) → lower error, faster convergence
  • Hardware: annular LED illuminator provides natural ring-based sampling
Non-uniform sampling Low-frequency energy concentration Annular illuminator RMS error Convergence speed

Why Sampling Distribution Matters

The energy distribution in the Fourier space of biological objects is highly non-uniform:

Performance Comparison

StrategyLED distributionRMS ErrorConvergence
Even grid (uniform)Regular square matrixHigherSlower
Uneven (annular rings)Dense center, sparse peripheryLowerFaster
💡 Intuition
Most of the "information" in a natural image lives at low spatial frequencies. Sampling more densely there — where it matters most — gives better reconstruction quality for the same total number of acquisitions. This is analogous to importance sampling in Monte Carlo methods.

Hardware Implementation

Annular FP Illuminator

  • Replaces the flat LED matrix with concentric LED rings
  • Inner ring: small angles → low-frequency patches (high energy region)
  • Outer ring: large angles → high-frequency patches (low energy region, fewer samples needed)
  • Better image quality on brain slices, blood smears, and pathology slides

QUESTION 17
Multilayer Modeling in FPM
FLASH MEMO
  • Standard FPM assumes a thin 2D object — breaks down for thick samples
  • Multi-slice model: object divided into n thin layers separated by Δz
  • Between layers: free-space propagation modeled by psf_t (Fresnel propagator)
  • FT{psf_t} = e^{√(k₀²−kx²−ky²)·Δz}
  • Enables recovery of images at different depths from same dataset
Multi-slice model Fresnel propagation Thick sample FPM Propagation kernel psf_t Depth-resolved imaging

Limitation of the 2D Model

The standard FPM model treats the sample as an infinitely thin 2D object:

I_n(x,y) = | ( O(x,y) · P_n(x,y) ) * PSF |² [2D model] Valid only when: sample thickness ≪ depth of field
⚠ Problem for Thick Samples
When the sample is tens of µm thick (e.g. tissue sections, organoids), the 2D model fails: different layers scatter and delay the wavefront differently. Ignoring this leads to reconstruction artifacts.

Multi-Slice (Multilayer) Model

The object is decomposed into n thin 2D slices, each separated by propagation distance Δz:

The Multi-Slice Field Propagation Formula

Field through slices: ( O₁(x,y) · P_n(x,y) ) * psf_t · O₂(x,y) * psf_t · O₃(x,y) * psf_t … Propagation kernel in Fourier space: FT{ psf_t } = e^{ √(k₀² − kx² − ky²) · Δz } k₀ = 2π/λ (wavenumber in medium) kx, ky = transverse spatial frequencies Δz = inter-slice distance
💡 Physical Meaning
The propagation kernel psf_t models how a wavefront diffractively spreads as it travels distance Δz in free space (Fresnel propagation). Multiplying by O_i at each slice applies the phase/amplitude modulation of that layer. This is a form of the beam propagation method (BPM).

Applications

Depth-resolved FPM

  • Reconstruct images at z = −10, −30, −50 µm
  • From the same set of raw LR acquisitions
  • Different cellular structures visible at each depth

Extension to Incoherent FPM

  • Fluorescence imaging: I_n = (O·P_n) * |PSF|²
  • Object and illumination pattern both unknown
  • Simultaneous recovery of speckle + object
  • Reconstructed resolution: 0.66 µm with 10× (0.25 NA)
Multiplexed Incoherent Imaging — Spectral & Depth Recovery

The multi-slice FPM concept extends to incoherent (fluorescence) imaging and can recover multiple hidden dimensions:

  • Super-resolution at multiple depths: from 2D monochrome data → HR images at multiple z-planes
  • Spectral recovery from monochrome: DMD projector generates colored speckle patterns; CCD records total intensity; algorithm recovers R, G, B channels separately
  • Approaches "ground truth" color image quality from a single monochrome sensor
